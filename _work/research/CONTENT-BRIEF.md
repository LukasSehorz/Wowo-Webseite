## 1 Executive summary (what the copy can safely say)

1. **What it is.** Formthotics are prefabricated, anatomically contoured insoles milled from a closed-cell polyethylene foam (Formax™), made in Christchurch, New Zealand, by Foot Science International (founded 1981). They contain no hard plastic shell. A trained professional heats them inside the customer's own shoe (3-minute heating cycle) and the foam then moulds to foot *and* shoe while the person stands and walks – one appointment, no plaster cast, no scan, no lab. They can be fine-tuned with stick-on wedges, heel raisers and metatarsal pads, and re-moulded later. (Sources: section 2.)
2. **What the research shows – honestly.** Formthotics are unusually well studied for a prefabricated insole: independent university groups (mainly La Trobe University, Melbourne) used them in several randomised trials. Consistent, well-replicated finding: they **redistribute load** – about **19–24 % lower peak pressure under the heel**, much more contact in the midfoot – and they still do this after 12+ months of wear. Clinical findings are **modest and condition-specific**: small short-term benefits for plantar heel pain (3 months), better than a cortisone injection at 12 weeks (difference below the threshold patients notice), a 34 % lower rate of typical overuse injuries in naval recruits that **did not reach statistical significance** (p = 0.098), and 36 % fewer falls in older people as part of a *package* of measures. **No benefit was found for chronic low-back pain** (RCT 2023) **or big-toe-joint osteoarthritis** (RCT 2022). (Sources: section 3.)
3. **What the copy must not say.** No promise of cure or freedom from pain, no "bewiesen/garantiert", no "sofortige Schmerzlinderung", no back-pain promise, no fear appeals. The manufacturer's own marketing language goes further than its evidence and further than German advertising law (HWG §3, §11) allows. Section 6 gives a phrase-by-phrase replacement list.
4. **Storyline that holds up.** Musculoskeletal disorders are the largest diagnosis group for sick days in BAuA's cross-insurer estimate (19.4 % of all sick days in 2024; rank 1–3 depending on the health insurer – section 4) → many employees stand or walk most of the day → the foot is the contact point to hard floors → a contoured, individually moulded insole measurably spreads the load → fitting takes minutes, done by physiotherapists who assess the person first → realistic expectations: comfort and load distribution yes, medical promises no.
5. **Important practical limitation for the B2B offer:** in certified safety footwear (EN ISO 20345) only insoles that were type-examined together with the shoe may be used (DGUV Regel 112-191). We found no evidence that Formthotics hold such approvals → see Open questions (section 7).

**Why no hard shell – arguments that are backed by data (use these):**
1. *Equivalence:* In the plantar-fasciitis RCT the firm-foam Formthotics performed the same as custom-made semi-rigid polypropylene orthoses (difference 1.3 points on a 100-point pain scale, 95 % CI −7.6 to 10.2) – Landorf 2006. A meta-analysis of 19 trials found "no difference at any time point" between customised and prefabricated orthoses – Whittaker 2018.
2. *Comfort and wear time:* In the naval trial recruits rated Formthotics as comfortable as a flat insole (70.8 vs 69.9 mm on a 100-mm scale) and wore them about 10 hours a day – Bonanno 2018. An insole only works when it is worn.
3. *Adaptability:* the foam can be re-heated, re-moulded, ground and combined with additions when the shoe or the complaint changes (MFR FAQ).

### 2.4 The thermoforming fitting process, step by step

Sources: MFR fitting guide https://www.formthotics.com/de_de/ausbildung-landing-page/leitfaden-fuer-die-individuelle-anpassung · MFR „6 Schritte“ https://www.formthotics.com/de_de/Medizinprodukte/formthotics-medizinisches-system/6-schritte · V20 manual (PDF above) · independent description in Bonanno 2018 (BJSM) and Whittaker 2020 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7526364/).

| # | Step | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Assessment** | In the medical setting the fitting is preceded by the „6 Tests“ (foot morphology & motion, alignment/gait, Jack's test, supination-resistance test, knee-bend test, neuromotor balance test) → choice of model, hardness and shape according to complaint, body weight/activity and **shoe type**. https://www.formthotics.com/de_de/Medizinprodukte/formthotics-medizinisches-system |
| 1 | Remove the existing insole | „Wenn möglich, sollten Sie die vorhandenen Einlegesohlen aus den Schuhen entfernen, um Platz für Formthotics zu schaffen.“ |
| 2 | Choose size and model | by shoe and foot size (sizes XS–XXL, some XXXL; youth and junior sizes) |
| 3 | Trim only if necessary | Use the shoe's original insole as the template, trim the **toe end only** – never the heel („Es ist nicht ratsam, die Ferse der Orthese zu kürzen“) |
| 4 | Insert and try on cold | Bend the sides, slide in, press the heel home; the person tries the shoes on *before* heating to check that nothing is too tight |
| 5 | **Heat inside the shoe** | Shoes (with the insoles in them) are placed upside-down on the **Formthotics V20 heating machine**, which blows warm air into the shoe. One button, one setting. **Heating cycle: 3 minutes** (blue LED); buzzer + flashing green LED = ready; the machine then **holds the temperature for a further 3 minutes**. (230 V / 800 W, CE-marked; for ski boots and other closed boots there is a „Boot Extension“.) No temperature value is published. |
| 6 | **Mould under body weight** | The person puts the warm shoes on and fastens them, stands hip-width, **knees slightly bent and over the second toe, toes pointing forward, holds for 30 seconds**. While cooling, the foam sets in this position. |
| 7 | Walk | A short walk completes the moulding and gives immediate feedback on comfort and fit. |
| 8 | Wear-in | MFR advice: ideally **one hour per day for the first 3–7 days**, then increase; shorter than with conventional orthoses. If pain, blisters or bruising occur: stop and have them re-checked. (In the naval RCT most side effects – blisters, arch pain – occurred in the first 2 weeks and were mild to moderate.) |
| 9 | Fine-tuning ("additions") | Stick-on or glued parts, added as needed: **rearfoot wedges** (medial/lateral, frontal-plane control), **forefoot / extended wedges**, **metatarsal domes (Pelotten)** for the transverse arch, **heel raisers** (4 mm / 6 mm / 3D ≈ 8 mm; Achilles/calf strain, leg-length difference), **arch pads**, wedged strips. The foam can also be ground and re-heated. |
| 10 | In-shoe test and follow-up | Re-test posture, gait and selected tests with the insoles in the shoe; review after **6 or 12 months**. |

- **Time required:** MFR: „In nur 3 Minuten wärmegeformt“ / „in wenigen Minuten angepasst“ / one visit („one visit solution“). Realistic for our copy: the *heating* takes 3 minutes; the whole appointment including assessment takes longer – do not advertise "3 Minuten" as the appointment length.
- **Who performs it:** medical range – „nur für medizinisches Fachpersonal“ (physiotherapists, podiatrists, doctors, orthopaedic shoe technicians); sports range – trained sports retailers. **[DE-Zitat]** distributor: „Überlassen Sie diese Entscheidung einfach Ihrem Therapeuten. Er weiß am besten, welche Einlage für Sie passend ist und hilft Ihnen bei der richtigen Auswahl, Anpassung und Justierung …“ and „Er begutachtet auch Ihre Schuhe … Denn nicht jeder Schuh ist für eine optimale Versorgung geeignet.“ (https://www.funktionelle-einlagen.de/)
- **Only with the dedicated heater:** the manual states that no other heating method is adequate.
- **Transferable:** between *similar* shoes; differently shaped shoes may need a second pair (MFR FAQ).
- Reference photos of every step: `_work/research/manufacturer-images/01-fitting-process/` (internal use only). MFR videos: https://vimeo.com/741724815 and others listed in SOURCES.md.

## 3 TASK B – Scientific evidence

**Where the manufacturer lists its research:** https://www.formthotics.com/de_de/ausbildung-landing-page/landing-page-der-wissensdatenbank/seite-mit-der-auflistung-von-forschungs-und-klinischen-daten (EN: https://www.formthotics.com/nz_en/training/knowledge-base/research-and-clinical-data) – 19 entries, each with a short abstract, a comment by the company's Medical Director Dr Bob Eckles (a podiatrist) and a gated download of the full paper. Further studies are cited in the "Medical Conditions" articles (heel pain, overuse injuries, knee, falls). We read the full texts hosted by the manufacturer and checked every paper independently in PubMed; additional Formthotics studies that the manufacturer does **not** list (including two negative trials) were found through a Europe PMC full-text search for "Formthotics" / "Foot Science International".

**Important for honest communication:** Of the 19 entries on the manufacturer's research page, only **10 entries (9 distinct studies – the falls trial appears twice) actually tested Formthotics**; the rest are general orthosis reviews, studies of other brands, validation studies of clinical tests, or lifestyle epidemiology. The manufacturer's headline claim – **[DE-Zitat]** „Unabhängige, von Experten begutachtete Studien haben bewiesen, dass Formthotics Schmerzen lindern, Verletzungen vorbeugen und die Ergebnisse der Patienten verbessern.“ (https://www.formthotics.com/de_de/ueber-uns/der-formthotics-unterschied) – overstates this body of evidence (see 3.5).

**Independence:** almost all trials were run by university groups with public/university funding; Foot Science International typically **donated the insoles** and had no role in design or analysis (stated in the papers). Exception: Baxter 2011 has a company co-author.

### 3.2 Study cards – studies that used Formthotics

#### S1 · Naval recruits – prevention of overuse injuries ★ best single trial
- **Citation:** Bonanno DR, Murley GS, Munteanu SE, Landorf KB, Menz HB. Effectiveness of foot orthoses for the prevention of lower limb overuse injuries in naval recruits: a randomised controlled trial. *Br J Sports Med.* 2018;52(5):298-302. DOI https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-098273 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29056595/ · Trial registry ACTRN12615000024549. MFR page: https://www.formthotics.com/nz_en/training/knowledge-base/research-and-clinical-data/effectiveness-of-foot-orthoses-for-the-prevention-of-lower-limb-overuse-injuries
- **Design:** participant- and assessor-blinded, parallel-group RCT, intention-to-treat. La Trobe University, Melbourne; Royal Australian Navy Recruit School.
- **Population:** 306 recruits (241 men, 65 women), 17–50 years, mean 22.2; 11 weeks of basic training.
- **Intervention / comparator:** full-length single-density (140 kg/m³) Formthotics, heat-moulded in athletic shoes and in Defence-issue boots (an Oliver fire-fighter boot), unmodified, n = 153 – versus a 3-mm **flat insole of the same foam, colour and branding** (credible sham), n = 153. Worn ≈ 10 hours/day in both groups.
- **Outcomes:** Primary – combined incidence of medial tibial stress syndrome (shin splints), patellofemoral pain, Achilles tendinopathy, plantar fasciitis/heel pain: **27/153 = 17.6 % vs 40/153 = 26.1 %**; incidence-rate ratio **0.66 (95 % CI 0.39–1.11), p = 0.098**; absolute risk reduction 8.5 percentage points; **NNT 12**. By injury (orthosis vs flat): shin splints 11 vs 14, patellofemoral 7 vs 14, heel pain 7 vs 12, Achilles 2 vs 0. Any lower-limb pain: 43.8 % vs 52.9 % (IRR 0.82, p = 0.121). No differences in pain severity, time to injury, lost training days (1.6 vs 1.0), shoe comfort (70.8 vs 69.9 mm at baseline). Physical health score (SF-12) slightly *lower* with orthoses (−1.8 points, p = 0.019). **Adverse events:** 20.3 % vs 12.4 % reported at least one (RR 1.63, p = 0.068; NNH 13) – arch pain, shin pain, blisters; 86 % mild/moderate, 77 % in the first two weeks. Cost of the insoles: AUD 35.
- **Takeaway:** Recruits who wore heat-moulded Formthotics had about one third fewer typical overuse injuries than recruits with flat look-alike insoles, but the trial was too small for this difference to be statistically certain. Minor, mostly temporary discomfort such as blisters was more common with the contoured insole.
- **Manufacturer claim vs paper:** MFR headline "Orthotics Reduce Overuse Injuries in Naval Recruits" and Dr Eckles' comment ("34 % lower frequency") omit in the headline that the result was **not statistically significant**; the abstract they reproduce does show p = 0.098, and another MFR article states it correctly ("Although the result was not statistically significant … the authors noted the effect was clinically relevant", https://www.formthotics.com/nz_en/training/knowledge-base/medical-conditions/preventing-overuse-injuries-with-load-management-and-orthotics). Numbers match.
- **Limitations:** young, fit, mostly male military population in boots – not office or retail staff; 11 weeks; four injuries pooled; sham insole may itself have some effect; insoles donated by the manufacturer (no other role); authors' own sample-size assumption (50 % reduction) was too optimistic.

#### S2 · Walking in work-type boots – pressure and shock ★ most relevant lab study for employees in boots
- **Citation:** Bonanno DR, Ledchumanasarma K, Landorf KB, Munteanu SE, Murley GS, Menz HB. Effects of a contoured foot orthosis and flat insole on plantar pressure and tibial acceleration while walking in defence boots. *Sci Rep.* 2019;9:1688. DOI https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-35830-5 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30737450/ · open access https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6368555/
- **Design:** within-subject laboratory study, randomised order, participants blinded; in-shoe pressure (pedar-X) and a tibial accelerometer.
- **Population:** 28 healthy adults (17 men, 11 women), walking ≈ 4.5 km/h.
- **Conditions:** boot only · boot + flat insole · boot + heat-moulded contoured orthosis (same foam; Foot Science International; the paper does not print the brand name – the devices are those of S1).
- **Outcomes (orthosis vs boot only):** rearfoot peak pressure 214.1 → 172.5 kPa = **−19 %** (p < 0.001, d = −1.07; flat insole 213.9 kPa = 0 %); rearfoot maximum force −9 %; medial midfoot contact area **+368 %** and force +334 % (load shifted to the arch); peak positive tibial acceleration 2.22 g → 2.07 g = **−6.8 %** (p = 0.018, small effect d = −0.19). Flat insole: no significant change at the heel, tibial acceleration +1.4 % (n.s.). Small pressure *increase* under the big toe with the orthosis (+9 %, p = 0.026). Comfort: no difference (63–66 mm).
- **Takeaway:** In stiff work-style boots the moulded insole took about a fifth of the peak pressure off the heel by letting the arch carry part of the load, and slightly reduced the shock travelling up the shin. A flat insole of the same material did neither.
- **Claim vs paper:** MFR summary correct ("reduced peak heel pressure … decreased tibial acceleration") but adds "which may translate to fewer overuse injuries" – that is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- **Limitations:** healthy young people, one session, immediate effects only, no symptoms measured; insoles donated by the manufacturer.

#### S3 · Plantar fasciitis – prefabricated vs custom vs sham, 12 months
- **Citation:** Landorf KB, Keenan AM, Herbert RD. Effectiveness of foot orthoses to treat plantar fasciitis: a randomized trial. *Arch Intern Med.* 2006;166(12):1305-1310. DOI https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.166.12.1305 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16801514/
- **Design:** pragmatic, participant-blinded, three-arm RCT, 12-month follow-up, intention-to-treat; University of Western Sydney.
- **Population:** 135 adults with plantar fasciitis ≥ 4 weeks (median 12 months), mean age ≈ 48, BMI ≈ 30–33.
- **Arms:** sham (soft thin EVA foam, n = 45) · **prefabricated ¾-length firm-foam Formthotic fitted per manufacturer's instructions (n = 44)** · custom semi-rigid polypropylene from a plaster cast (n = 46).
- **Outcomes (Foot Health Status Questionnaire, 0–100, higher = better):** Pain, mean: sham 45.1 → 63.4 (3 mo) → 82.3 (12 mo); **Formthotics 42.1 → 71.4 → 83.8**; custom 48.4 → 71.8 → 83.1. Adjusted difference vs sham at 3 months: pain +8.7 (95 % CI −0.1 to 17.6; p = 0.05) for prefabricated, +7.4 (p = 0.10) for custom; function **+8.4 (1.0–15.8; p = 0.03)** and +7.5 (p = 0.04). **At 12 months no significant differences.** Prefabricated vs custom: 1.3 points (n.s.). NNT for a relevant functional improvement at 3 months: 6 (prefabricated), 4 (custom). Cost then: USD 45–90 vs 225+.
- **Takeaway:** Everyone got better over a year, with or without a real orthosis; people with Formthotics (or custom orthoses) improved somewhat faster in the first three months. The inexpensive heat-fitted insole did as well as the custom-made hard-shell device.
- **Claim vs paper:** MFR reproduces abstract correctly, including "do not have long-term beneficial effects compared with a sham device". Its marketing sentence "long-term improvements in pain and function" (quality blog) is **not** supported by this trial.
- **Limitations:** the short-term pain effect just missed significance; effects are small; prefabricated group heavier at baseline; orthoses donated (sponsors had no role); trial ran 1999–2001.

#### S4 · SOOTHE – insoles vs cortisone injection for heel pain ★ working-age adults
- **Citation:** Whittaker GA, Munteanu SE, Menz HB, Gerrard JM, Elzarka A, Landorf KB. Effectiveness of foot orthoses versus corticosteroid injection for plantar heel pain: the SOOTHE randomized clinical trial. *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther.* 2019;49(7):491-500. DOI https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2019.8807 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31130060/ · Protocol (open access, names the device): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5936710/ · Secondary analysis with baseline table: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7526364/
- **Design:** assessor-blinded parallel-group RCT, La Trobe University; both groups also received stretching and footwear advice.
- **Population:** 103 adults, 21–72 years (mean ≈ 43–45), 63 women, BMI ≈ 30, **on their feet ≈ 7 hours a day**, heel pain for a median of 6 months.
- **Arms:** full-length dual-density Formthotics (base Shore A 50, top Shore A 25), heat-moulded, n = 53 – vs one ultrasound-guided corticosteroid injection, n = 50.
- **Outcomes (FHSQ foot pain, 0–100; baseline 38.4 vs 38.5):** week 4 – injection better by **8.2 points** (95 % CI 0.6–15.8); week 12 – **orthoses better by 8.5 points** (0.2–16.8). Both statistically significant, both **below the minimal important difference of 12.5 points**.
- **Takeaway:** A cortisone injection eased heel pain faster, but after three months the people with heat-moulded insoles were doing slightly better. The differences were small enough that patients might not notice them – so a non-invasive insole is a reasonable alternative, not a miracle.
- **Claim vs paper:** MFR heel-pain article states this correctly, including the footnote that the difference "did not meet criteria for clinically meaningful difference" (https://www.formthotics.com/nz_en/training/knowledge-base/medical-conditions/managing-plantar-heel-pain-evidence-based-care).
- **Limitations:** no placebo arm; within-group follow-up means could not be checked (full text paywalled – numbers above are from the PubMed abstract); 12 weeks; insoles supplied free by the manufacturer (no role).

#### S5 · Standing – pressure under the heel with different inserts ★ relevant to standing work
- **Citation:** Chia JKK, Suresh S, Kuah A, Ong JLJ, Phua JMT, Seah AL. Comparative trial of the foot pressure patterns between corrective orthotics, formthotics, bone spur pads and flat insoles in patients with chronic plantar fasciitis. *Ann Acad Med Singapore.* 2009;38(10):869-875. DOI https://doi.org/10.47102/annals-acadmedsg.v38n10p869 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19890578/ · free PDF https://www.annals.edu.sg/pdf/38VolNo10Oct2009/V38N10p869.pdf
- **Design:** controlled, non-blinded comparison in a Singapore sports-medicine clinic; pressure mat, **quiet standing in shoes**.
- **Population:** 30 adults (20–65 y) with one-sided chronic plantar fasciitis (pain 5.9/10, symptoms ≈ 31 weeks).
- **Conditions:** shoe only · flat insole · viscoelastic heel ("bone spur") pad · **full-length firm Formthotics** · custom orthosis with heel aperture.
- **Outcomes, painful foot, rearfoot peak pressure (N/cm²):** 10.36 · 9.74 · 10.45 · **7.90** · 7.30 (p = 0.0008) → Formthotics **−24 %**, custom −30 %, flat insole −6 %, heel pad +1 %. Rearfoot share of contact area 59 % → 69 % with Formthotics. Similar pattern on the healthy foot (11.43 → 8.16).
- **Takeaway:** While simply standing, the moulded insole lowered the peak pressure under the painful heel by about a quarter; a soft heel cushion did not lower it at all. A purpose-built custom orthosis was slightly better still.
- **Claim vs paper:** MFR comment concedes the custom device was best, then speculates "we suspect that if the device had not been so specifically constructed, Formthotics would have been superior" – speculation, ignore. The authors note orthoses "may be of particular use in patients whose occupation entails extended periods of standing" (their opinion, not a tested result).
- **Limitations:** single session, no pain outcome, static measurement, not blinded, small.

#### S6 · Do they still work after a year of wear? ★ durability
- **Citation:** Cronkwright DG, Spink MJ, Landorf KB, Menz HB. Evaluation of the pressure-redistributing properties of prefabricated foot orthoses in older people after at least 12 months of wear. *Gait Posture.* 2011;34(4):553-557. DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gaitpost.2011.07.016 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21855344/ · Conference abstract with the percentages (open access): *J Foot Ankle Res* 2011;4(Suppl 1):O13, https://doi.org/10.1186/1757-1146-4-S1-O13
- **Design / population:** in-shoe pressure while walking; 31 adults > 65 y (mean 75.4; 21 women) from the BMJ falls trial; shoe only vs new vs **own pair worn ≥ 12 months** ("full length, dual-density prefabricated Formthotic™ devices").
- **Outcomes vs shoe only:** heel peak pressure **new −23 %, worn −18 %**; midfoot force +42 % / +44 %; midfoot contact area +51 % / +53 %. Worn vs new: heel peak pressure +6 % (p = 0.001), heel force +5 %, forefoot force +2 % – "only slightly less effective".
- **Takeaway:** After more than a year of daily use the insoles still spread the load almost as well as a brand-new pair. This was measured in older, lighter-duty users – heavy daily use at work will wear them faster.
- **Claim vs paper:** MFR page quotes the abstract correctly; Dr Eckles himself adds the caveat about older, less demanding users. Note the MFR page links the conference abstract, the full paper exists (above).
- **Limitations:** older people only; pressure, not symptoms; no information on hours worn.

#### S7 · Four heel inserts compared in older people with heel pain
- **Citation:** Bonanno DR, Landorf KB, Menz HB. Pressure-relieving properties of various shoe inserts in older people with plantar heel pain. *Gait Posture.* 2011;33(3):385-389. DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gaitpost.2010.12.009 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21256025/
- **Design / population:** in-shoe pressure while walking, 36 adults > 65 y with heel pain; silicone heel cup · soft foam heel pad · heel lift · prefabricated contoured orthosis.
- **Outcome:** three of four inserts lowered heel peak pressure; the prefabricated orthosis by far the most – "a fivefold reduction compared to the next most effective insert" – and it was the only insert that did not increase forefoot pressure.
- **Takeaway:** For taking load off a sore heel, a contoured insole beat heel cushions and heel lifts by a wide margin. It works by letting the arch share the load rather than by padding the heel.
- **Status: PARTIALLY VERIFIED** – the abstract does not name the brand; the same group's SOOTHE protocol states that the Formthotics "has been used in previous PHP research" citing this paper. Exact percentages are in the paywalled full text – do not quote numbers other than "fivefold".

#### S8 · Falls in older people (BMJ)
- **Citation:** Spink MJ, Menz HB, Fotoohabadi MR, Wee E, Landorf KB, Hill KD, Lord SR. Effectiveness of a multifaceted podiatry intervention to prevent falls in community dwelling older people with disabling foot pain: randomised controlled trial. *BMJ.* 2011;342:d3411. DOI https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d3411 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21680622/ · open access https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3116775/
- **Design / population:** parallel-group RCT, 305 people ≥ 65 y (mean 74) with disabling foot pain and raised fall risk; 12 months.
- **Intervention:** heat-moulded dual-density Formthotics (customised with Poron padding) **plus** footwear advice and an AUD 100 footwear voucher, a home foot-and-ankle exercise programme, a falls-education booklet and routine podiatry – vs routine podiatry only.
- **Outcomes:** 103 vs 161 falls; 0.67 vs 1.06 falls per person-year; **IRR 0.64 (95 % CI 0.45–0.91), p = 0.01 → 36 % fewer falls**. Proportion who fell at all: RR 0.85 (n.s.); fractures 1 vs 7 (p = 0.07). 55 % wore the orthoses "most of the time". No adverse events reported.
- **Takeaway:** A programme that combined moulded insoles with better shoes, foot exercises and education cut the number of falls by about a third. The trial cannot tell how much of that was due to the insoles.
- **Claim vs paper:** MFR headline "Orthotics Reduce Falls in Seniors with Foot Pain" and the German FAQ sentence „Formthotics verringern Stürze …“ attribute the effect to the insoles – **not supported**; Dr Eckles' comment is fairer ("along with exercise recommendations … a moderate reduction").
- **Relevance for our site:** low (older people, not employees). Funding: NHMRC (public); orthoses provided by the manufacturer.

#### S9 · Flat-arched feet – muscle activity
- **Citation:** Murley GS, Landorf KB, Menz HB. Do foot orthoses change lower limb muscle activity in flat-arched feet towards a pattern observed in normal-arched feet? *Clin Biomech.* 2010;25(7):728-736. DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinbiomech.2010.05.001 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20538390/ (brand named in the data table of Banwell et al. 2014, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4108129/: "Formthotic® dual density polyethylene foam").
- **Design / population:** laboratory EMG (fine-wire and surface) during walking; 30 symptom-free young adults with radiographically confirmed flat-arched feet; barefoot · shoe · heat-moulded, modified prefabricated orthosis · 20° inverted custom orthosis.
- **Outcomes vs shoe only:** tibialis posterior peak amplitude at contact **−19 %** (p = 0.007; custom −12 %); peroneus longus in midstance/propulsion **+21 %** (p = 0.024) – only the prefabricated device moved peroneus activity towards the normal-arch pattern.
- **Takeaway:** In people with flat feet the moulded insole measurably changed how hard two key stabilising muscles had to work, nudging the pattern towards that of normal-arched feet. Whether this prevents or relieves complaints was not tested.
- **Discrepancy:** the MFR hosts the 2011 conference abstract, which gives +19 %/+14 % for peroneus longus; the final paper reports +21 %/+16 %. Use the journal figures.

#### S10 · One-hour run – fatigue (athletes)
- **Citation:** Kelly LA, Girard O, Racinais S. Effect of orthoses on changes in neuromuscular control and aerobic cost of a 1-h run. *Med Sci Sports Exerc.* 2011;43(12):2335-2343. DOI https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e31822037ca · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21552159/
- **Design / population:** counterbalanced crossover, ASPETAR Qatar; 12 recreational athletes; 1 h treadmill with vs without heat-moulded Formthotics ("custom-molded foot orthoses (Formthotics; Foot Science International)").
- **Outcomes:** oxygen consumption unchanged; **heart rate −3 %** (p < 0.02); lower EMG amplitude of vastus medialis (−13.3 %) and medial gastrocnemius (−10.7 %); loss of rapid force production (rate of torque development, first 200 ms) after the run **−6 % with vs −33 % without** orthoses (p < 0.01). Maximal strength fell equally in both conditions (−9 %).
- **Takeaway:** Runners' calf muscles lost less of their "snap" after an hour's run when they wore the moulded insoles, and heart rate was marginally lower. The energy cost of running did not change – no performance promise.
- **Limitations:** 12 people, one run each, not blinded; insoles donated.

#### S11 · Danish conscripts – back and leg problems
- **Citation:** Larsen K, Weidich F, Leboeuf-Yde C. Can custom-made biomechanic shoe orthoses prevent problems in the back and lower extremities? A randomized, controlled intervention trial of 146 military conscripts. *J Manipulative Physiol Ther.* 2002;25(5):326-331. DOI https://doi.org/10.1067/mmt.2002.124419 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12072853/
- **Device:** described in the BJSM meta-analysis (G1) as "Prefabricated polyethylene orthosis (Formthotics). Heat moulded (n = 77)" vs shoe alone (n = 69).
- **Outcomes:** intention-to-treat – any back/leg problem 40 % vs 56 % (RR 0.7, ARR 15 %, p = 0.114, n.s.); shin splints **RR 0.3, ARR 18 %, NNT 6 (significant)**. "Actual-use" analysis significant for total problems (RR 0.7, NNT 5); worst-case analysis: no significant differences. Authors: "the choice of statistical approach determined the outcome."
- **Takeaway:** Conscripts with heat-moulded insoles had clearly fewer shin splints; the overall drop in back and leg problems was not statistically robust.
- **Status: PARTIALLY VERIFIED** (brand from the systematic review, not from the abstract). Not on the MFR research page. Unblinded, no sham.

#### S12 · NZ Army screening protocol – *do not use*
- **Citation:** Baxter ML, Baycroft C, Baxter GD. Lower limb injuries in soldiers: feasibility of reduction through implementation of a novel orthotic screening protocol. *Mil Med.* 2011;176(3):291-296. DOI https://doi.org/10.7205/MILMED-D-10-00352 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21456355/
- **What it is:** non-randomised feasibility study: 102 recruits screened, 47 of them issued dual-density Formthotics (**not heat-moulded**, "because of time constraints"), compared with injury records of 807 randomly drawn serving soldiers. Reports significant reductions in 7 of 9 injury categories, but no usable group counts.
- **Why not to use:** apples-and-oranges comparison (recruits vs regular force, different data sources), no blinding, **co-author Charles Baycroft is the co-founder and affiliated with Foot Science International**. MFR describes it as "900+ participants … statistically significant reduction" – technically true, scientifically weak.

#### S13 · Chronic low-back pain – negative RCT (not mentioned by the manufacturer)
- **Citation:** Sadler S, Spink M, Lanting S, Chuter V. A randomised controlled trial investigating the effect of foot orthoses for the treatment of chronic nonspecific low back pain. *Musculoskeletal Care.* 2023;21(3):856-864. DOI https://doi.org/10.1002/msc.1762 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36951154/ · Protocol naming Formthotics: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6192308/
- **Design:** RCT, 41 adults; prefabricated orthoses + "The Back Book" (n = 20) vs "The Back Book" only (n = 21); 12 weeks.
- **Outcomes:** pain −0.84 (95 % CI −2.09 to 0.41; p = 0.18); function −1.47 (−5.51 to 2.57; p = 0.47) – **no significant benefit**. Recruitment fell short of the planned 60.
- **Takeaway:** In the only randomised trial of these insoles for chronic back pain, they did not help more than an education booklet alone. The trial was small, so a modest effect cannot be ruled out – but there is no positive evidence either.
- **Consequence:** the MFR indication „Schmerzen im unteren Rückenbereich“ and the distributor's „Rückenbereich“ wording must **not** be turned into a promise on our site.

#### S14–S16 · Further trials with Formthotics (not on the manufacturer's page)
- **S14 Paterson KL et al.** Foot orthoses vs sham insoles for first-MTP-joint osteoarthritis (FORT). *Osteoarthritis Cartilage.* 2022;30(7):956-964. DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joca.2022.01.014 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35272050/ · protocol naming "Blue Formthotic™ … 140 kg/m³": https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7726603/. Result as summarised by the 2024 Cochrane review (https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007809.pub3): 88 participants; pain 3.5 vs 3.9 (0–10) – "little or no difference in pain, function, or quality of life compared to sham inserts (moderate certainty)".
- **S15 Menz HB et al.** *Arthritis Care Res.* 2016;68(5):581-589. DOI https://doi.org/10.1002/acr.22750 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26638878/ (protocol naming Formthotics: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3995518/). 102 people with big-toe-joint OA: insoles and rocker-sole shoes reduced pain equally (difference 2.05 points, p = 0.477); insoles were worn more (448 vs 287 h) and caused fewer side effects (16 % vs 39 %).
- **S16 King MG et al.** *Physiother Res Int.* 2024;29(4):e2118. DOI https://doi.org/10.1002/pri.2118 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39132922/. Feasibility RCT, 27 people with hip OA, telehealth-prescribed contoured insoles vs flat inserts, 6 weeks: +2,590 steps/day (95 % CI 260–4,920). Exploratory only.
- **Also:** Formthotics were part of the 12-week non-surgical programme (education, exercise, diet, insoles, pain medication) in the Danish knee-osteoarthritis trials by Skou et al. (protocols https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3461437/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3533085/) – effect of the insoles alone unknown. Used as "industry standard comparator" in a 2025 footwear study in women with heel pain (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12163246/). In a survey of 264 podiatrists (UK 124, Australia 79, NZ 32) Formthotics was the most frequently named prefabricated brand in Australia and (with Footbionics) New Zealand for rheumatoid arthritis – "despite a current lack of evidence suggesting that any specific prefabricated FO is more effective than any other" (Chapman 2019, https://doi.org/10.1186/s13047-019-0314-5).

### 3.3 Evidence on foot orthoses in general (not brand-specific – must be labelled as such, HWG §6 no. 2)

- **G1 · Bonanno DR, Landorf KB, Munteanu SE, Murley GS, Menz HB.** Effectiveness of foot orthoses and shock-absorbing insoles for the prevention of injury: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Br J Sports Med.* 2017;51(2):86-96. DOI https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096671 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27919918/. 11 orthosis trials + 7 shock-absorbing-insole trials, mostly military recruits; trial quality low to moderate (PEDro median 5/10 and 3/10). **Foot orthoses: overall injuries RR 0.72 (0.55–0.94) = −28 %; stress fractures RR 0.59 (0.45–0.76) = −41 %; soft-tissue injuries RR 0.79 (n.s.). Shock-absorbing insoles: overall RR 0.92 (0.73–1.16), stress fractures 1.15, soft tissue 0.92 – no effect.** Takeaway: contoured, supporting insoles lowered injury rates in physically demanding training; insoles that merely cushion did not. One included orthosis trial used Formthotics (S11).
- **G2 · Whittaker GA et al.** Foot orthoses for plantar heel pain: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Br J Sports Med.* 2018;52(5):322-328. DOI https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-097355 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28935689/. 19 RCTs, 1,660 participants. Short term (0–6 wk): no benefit (very low quality); **medium term (7–12 wk): less pain than sham, SMD −0.27 (−0.48 to −0.06), moderate-quality evidence**, clinical importance uncertain; long term: no benefit; **customised vs prefabricated: no difference at any time point**.
- **G3 · Pires Neves M et al.** Effects of foot orthoses on pain and the prevention of lower limb injuries in runners: systematic review and meta-analysis. *J Sport Rehabil.* 2022;31(8):1067-1074. DOI https://doi.org/10.1123/jsr.2021-0302 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35894921/. 12 RCTs, 5,321 participants; injuries 238 (24 %) with orthoses vs 721 (37 %) in controls; RR 0.6 (0.5–0.7), 7 studies, n = 2,983, moderate-quality evidence. MFR quotes this correctly as "40 % reduction" (overuse-injury article). Caveat: despite the title, many included trials are in military recruits.
- **Guidelines cited by the MFR (correctly):** APTA/JOSPT clinical practice guideline *Heel Pain – Plantar Fasciitis: Revision 2023* (Koc TA Jr et al., *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther.* 2023;53(12):CPG1-CPG39, https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2023.0303) – orthoses as an optional adjunct, especially with excessive pronation or positive response to anti-pronation taping; *BJSM* best-practice guide (Morrissey D et al. 2021;55:1106-1118, https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-101970) – core approach is stretching, taping and education; orthoses or shock-wave therapy considered only after ≈ 4–6 weeks. → Insoles are an adjunct to active physiotherapy, which fits our client's profession.
- **Older narrative review:** Hume P et al. *Sports Med.* 2008;38(9):759-779 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18712943/): effects of orthoses "mostly trivial"; moderate effects for plantar fasciitis and tibial stress fractures with customised semi-rigid devices. Shows the field is not uniformly positive.

## 6 Heilmittelwerbegesetz (HWG) – wording guide

*Not legal advice – have the final copy checked by a lawyer for Heilmittelwerberecht. Statute text read on 2026-09-17: https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/heilmwerbg/ (§§ 1, 3, 6, 7, 11, 12).* **Why it applies:** the HWG covers advertising for medical devices (§1 (1) no. 1a – the manufacturer declares the medical range a Class I device) and for any object or treatment once the advertising refers to relieving complaints (§1 (1) no. 2). HR managers and employees are *outside* professional circles → the strict consumer rules of §11 apply.

| Rule | Prohibits | Consequence for our pages |
|---|---|---|
| §3 no. 1 | Attributing effects the product does not demonstrably have | Claim only what section 3 supports: nothing on back, knee, hip, posture „correction“, fewer sick days. |
| §3 no. 2a | Impression that success is certain | No „garantiert“, „bewiesen“, „sofortige Schmerzlinderung“, „schmerzfrei“; no efficacy guarantee. |
| §3 no. 2b | Impression that no harmful effects occur | No „ohne Nebenwirkungen“; mention the wear-in phase (S1: more blisters/arch discomfort in the first two weeks). |
| §3 no. 3a | Misleading statements about the nature of the product | Not „maßgefertigt“ / „orthopädische Maßeinlage“ → „vorgefertigt, individuell thermisch angepasst“. |
| **§6 no. 2–3** (citing studies) | Referring to publications without showing whether they concern the advertised product and without author, date and source; altered quotes, tables or figures | Every study mention: **author – journal – year** + „untersucht wurden Formthotics“ **or** „betrifft Einlagen verschiedener Hersteller, nicht speziell Formthotics“ (G1–G3, all of section 4). Charts with the published numbers only, no truncated axes. |
| §11 (1) no. 7 (applies to devices) | Suggesting health suffers without the product or (normal) health improves with it | No fear appeals (✗ „Wer den ganzen Tag steht, riskiert ohne Einlagen bleibende Schäden“); no promise that healthy staff become „gesünder“. |
| §11 (1) no. 9, 11 | Advertising not recognisable as such; misleading third-party statements | Evidence pages must be visibly part of a commercial site. Testimonials only about service and comfort, never about healing. |
| §11 (1) no. 2, 3, 5 (apply to the *treatment* we advertise) | Recommendations by scientists/health professionals; case histories; before/after or disease imagery | ✗ „von Ärzten und Physiotherapeuten empfohlen“, ✗ Dr Eckles quotes, ✗ the manufacturer's patient cases and athlete stories, ✗ before/after foot photos. The owners presenting their own qualification is fine. |
| §7 | Gifts/benefits tied to the product (exceptions: trifles, fixed cash or quantity discounts, customary accessories, advice) | Selling vouchers to employers is not a gift; free trial vouchers, raffles, „2 für 1“, incentives for HR contacts → legal check first. |
| §12 + annex | Public advertising relating to listed diseases | Not touched by musculoskeletal topics; do not pick up the manufacturer's diabetes/leprosy cases. |
